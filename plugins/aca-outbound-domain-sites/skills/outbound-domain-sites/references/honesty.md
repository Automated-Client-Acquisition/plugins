# Honesty rules

Copying the *structure* of a good sales page is fine. Copying or inventing its
*claims* is not.

This is not a style preference. Fifteen domains repeating a fabricated
statistic is an advertising-standards problem in most jurisdictions, and it is
the fastest way to get the whole set cross-referenced and blocklisted the
moment one person compares two of the pages.

## Never generate

- **Testimonials.** Not "representative", not "illustrative", not with invented
  names, companies or headshots.
- **Client logos.** Using a real company's mark without permission is
  trademark infringement and implies a relationship that does not exist.
- **Ratings and review counts.** "4.8/5 from 200 reviews" is verifiable and
  false.
- **Performance metrics.** Reply rates, meetings booked, revenue attributed,
  deliverability percentages — unless the user supplied them and can evidence
  them.
- **Case studies.** Including anonymised ones. "A SaaS client saw 40 meetings
  in 6 weeks" is a claim about a real outcome.
- **Team size, founding date, client count** — small facts, equally checkable.

If the user asks you to add any of these and cannot evidence them, say plainly
that you will not, offer the alternative below, and move on. Do not negotiate.

## Always require provenance

For every number that goes on a page, record what it measures and where it came
from, and render that on the page:

```
0.81%   Bounce rate
        Measured across 94,281 sends
```

The footnote is not clutter. It is what separates a number a reader believes
from one they discount.

## When there is no proof yet

This is common and entirely workable. Sell the method rather than the results:

- **Process** — what actually happens, step by step
- **Guarantees** — what the user carries instead of the client
- **Response commitments** — how fast, and who answers
- **Ownership** — what the client keeps at the end
- **Pricing structure** — a pay-per-outcome model is itself a strong claim,
  because it is a commitment rather than a boast

These are statements about how the user works. They are true on day one.

## Label illustrative UI

Product mockups are fine and make a page feel real. Anything inside them that
resembles a metric must be labelled:

> *Illustrative portal view. Your numbers replace these on day one.*

Same for an example email — mark it as an example, not a client's message.

## Commercial promises are contracts

"No-shows are not billed." "Free pilot." "We install it in-house at day 180."
"100 to 400 meetings."

Once printed on fifteen live domains these are representations a customer can
hold the user to. Before publishing, ask directly:

> If a prospect signed off this page, would the terms on it be the deal they get?

If the answer is no, change the page — not the contract.

## Estimates and calculators

A calculator that projects revenue must:

- compute only from inputs the visitor supplied
- state on screen that it is an estimate, not a forecast or guarantee
- never move in a direction that misleads — check that raising any input
  changes the output the way a reader would expect

If you cannot state a price honestly (because it depends on scope), do not
invent a range. Say what it depends on and offer a written quote. "Whatever we
quote has to come in under £X, or it is not worth doing" is more persuasive
than a made-up number, and it is true.
